## Monday morning

[Case 1](../case-1-nishas-ai-team/a-company-of-one.md) ended with a choice. By Monday morning, Nisha had to decide how to spend her evenings. She could write her book, change her method, or take a paid job for a foreign client.

She chose the paid job.

It came through her mentor. A small US company, Northfield Home Goods (a fictional name), wanted to buy a share in a textile exporter in Faisalabad, Pakistan. The exporter, Sutlej Weavers (also fictional), reported its accounts under IFRS, the international accounting rules used in Pakistan and many other countries. Northfield's board thought in US GAAP, the American accounting rules. Before its meeting on Friday morning, the board wanted to know one thing: what would the exporter's profit look like under US GAAP?

Mark Ellis, Northfield's finance director, wrote to Nisha on Monday evening.

*"We do not need a full conversion. We need a short bridge, from IFRS profit to US GAAP profit, for three areas: inventory, leases and development costs. The board meets Friday at 9 a.m. New York time. I need the file by Thursday, 6 p.m. New York time. The fee is fixed: US$2,500, paid on delivery."*

Nisha did the sum in her head. US$2,500 was about 700,000 Pakistani rupees (Rs 700,000). That was more than a month of her rent. And if this went well, Mark said, there could be five more jobs like it.

There was one problem. Nisha still had her day job. She could work on Mark's job only in the evenings, from Monday to Thursday. Four evenings. She was also still learning US GAAP.

## What a bridge is

A **bridge** (also called a reconciliation) starts with a number under one set of rules and walks, one step at a time, to the same number under the other set of rules. Each step is one difference between the rules, with its amount.

Nisha already knew the three areas Mark had named were places where IFRS and US GAAP disagree:

- **Inventory.** When the value of goods falls, both sets of rules write the goods down. If the value later recovers, IFRS requires the write-down to be reversed, up to the original cost. US GAAP does not allow the reversal[^inventory] of a write-down made at a year-end.[^us-gaap-new-cost]
- **Development costs.** Under IFRS, some costs of developing a new product or process must be recorded as an asset when strict tests are met. Under US GAAP, development costs are generally treated as an expense when they are spent.[^development]
- **Leases.** For the company that rents an asset, such as a warehouse, the two sets of rules show the yearly cost differently. Under US GAAP, the cost of a usual (operating) lease is one even amount each year, on a single line.[^leases] Under US GAAP there are two kinds of lease. For a finance lease, the cost follows a pattern close to IFRS. Which kind a lease is depends on its contract.

> **You can skip this box.** Under IFRS, the same lease cost is split into depreciation (the asset's cost spread over its life) and interest. That split makes the cost higher in the early years and lower later.

Each difference was simple to say. Finding every place it touched Sutlej's accounts, and getting the amounts right, was the work.

## The brief

On Monday night, Nisha did not open Claude first. She opened a blank page and wrote a brief, in the four parts she had learned.

| Part | What Nisha wrote |
|---|---|
| **Outcome** | A bridge from Sutlej Weavers' IFRS profit before tax to US GAAP profit before tax, for inventory, leases and development costs only. Done when every adjustment has an amount, the IFRS treatment, the US GAAP treatment and a source, and every assumption is listed. |
| **Context** | For Mark's board. Only the files in the project: Sutlej's trial balance, its notes on inventory, leases and development costs, and Mark's engagement letter. Official or well-known sources for the rules. |
| **Format** | One table, in the order of the three areas, with a total. A short list of open questions under it. List what you could not check. |
| **Constraints** | Plain English that a board member who is not an accountant can read. Prepare and check only. Never send, share or publish anything. Never contact Mark or Sutlej. |

The last line mattered most to her. In Case 1, she had been in the room every minute. This time, the work would happen at night, while she slept.

## Tuesday evening

On Tuesday evening, Nisha came home at seven and gave herself two hours to build her AI Worker.

In Case 1, she had used the older kind of Claude project, which works like a folder: one place for one conversation and its files. This time she used a **rebuilt project**. It is one conversation, the **coordinator**, that hands each job to its own **thread**. Each thread works at its own borrowed desk.[^projects]

She opened **Projects** at claude.ai/code and selected **New project**. The same projects are in the Code tab of the Claude desktop app.[^projects-where]

She set it up in five steps ([Exhibit 2](exhibits.md#exhibit-2-the-project-setup)).

> **Doing the lab?** Open the exhibits only after you finish it. They show what Nisha found.

1. **The brief became the project instructions.** In **Project settings > Memory > Project instructions**, she pasted her four-part brief. Every new thread would start with it.[^standing]
2. **The files went to the Library.** With **Add** on the **Library** tab, she uploaded Sutlej's trial balance, its three notes and Mark's engagement letter. The Library sits outside every desk, so the files would still be there after a desk was cleared. Only deleting the whole project would remove them, together with its threads and its memory.[^delete]

   > **A Library is not a business record.** For drafts and for learning, the Library is the right place. A business AI Worker keeps its progress, its approvals and its accepted results in governed business records, where each record has an owner and a history of every change. A project Library is not that kind of system. [From personal worker to business worker](../architecture/from-personal-worker-to-business-worker.md) explains the difference.

3. **One thread for each area.** She did not start the threads herself. She told the coordinator: *"Start one thread for each area: inventory, development costs and leases. Each prepares its part of the bridge."* Claude started three threads, and each one appeared as a card under her message.[^projects]
4. **A reviewer thread.** In the same message she asked for a fourth thread with one job: to review the other three and grade the bridge, as Claude had graded its own work in Case 1. It was told not to change anything.
5. **A nightly run.** She asked the project to run the job every night at 1 a.m.: refresh the bridge from the files, and save a dated version to the Library. When you ask a project for scheduled work, Claude creates a scheduled task that runs as threads in that project. It appeared on the project's **Scheduled tasks** tab.[^projects]

## A brief for a stranger

Writing the nightly instruction took longer than she expected. Outside a project, each scheduled run is a new session. Inside a project, a run can continue an earlier thread instead.[^continue] Either way, she could not count on the run remembering Tuesday's conversation, and no one would be awake to answer its questions.[^routines] So her instruction had to stand alone, as if she were writing for a stranger who would arrive at 1 a.m. with no one to ask.

She wrote: *"Using the project instructions and only the files in the Library, update the bridge. Save it to the Library as bridge with tonight's date. At the top, list what changed since the last version, and every assumption you made. Do not send, share or publish anything."*

## What the worker could reach

Then Nisha did something she had not done in Case 1. She made a list of what her worker could reach, and what it could not ([Exhibit 6](exhibits.md#exhibit-6-the-permission-map)).

The threads could read the files in the Library and save new files there. They could look up rules on the web only where their network allowed, which is explained below. That was all.

Then she found a surprise. Connectors, the apps linked to Claude, belong to her whole Claude account, not to one project. Every cloud thread can use all of them, and the coordinator itself has none.[^connectors] A new scheduled task also includes all of her connectors unless she removes them.[^task-connectors] So "not in this project" was not a choice she could make. She disconnected Gmail from her account, so that **no** app that could send an email or share a file was connected. Then she opened the scheduled task and emptied its connector list. That list is a default for the task. Anthropic's docs do not say it limits the threads a project starts, so the real lock was the missing Gmail.

The threads ran in the **Default** cloud environment. Its network setting is **Limited**, with **Allow package managers** checked. Older screens call this setting **Trusted**. It lets a desk's own network reach only a list of common developer sites.[^environment] It does not limit connectors, which reach their services through Anthropic's servers.

Sites such as KPMG's or Deloitte's are probably not on that list, so a lookup there would likely fail with a "host not allowed" error. Nisha did not count on the web. She added a short summary of the three rules, with their sources, to the project instructions, and told the threads to work from it and from the Library files.

She had a reason for disconnecting Gmail. Anything a scheduled task does through her connectors appears as her, on every plan.[^routines-identity] An email sent at 1 a.m. by her AI Worker would look, to Mark, exactly like an email from Nisha.

Her own rule was simple: *"Only I send things to the client."* She wrote it into the project instructions. But she knew from Case 1 that a rule in a chat is only words. So she also made sure the worker had no way to send anything at all.

At nine, she closed her laptop. The first run would start at one.

## An email at seven

Nisha got home on Thursday at seven. There were two emails waiting.

The first was from Sutlej's accountant. The rent was Rs 10.0 million a year. The engagement letter had a typing error. The Rs 2.0 million difference in inventory was goods in transit, which he would explain on Monday. The lease contract would come tomorrow.

The second was from Mark ([Exhibit 7](exhibits.md#exhibit-7-the-thursday-request-and-three-draft-replies)).

From now on, could it email me its nightly update directly? Then I see progress every morning without waiting for you. If this works, I have five more targets to look at this quarter."*

6 p.m. in New York was 4 a.m. on Friday in Karachi. Nisha had the whole night. But the lease contract would not come tonight.

## What the lease line depended on

She opened the bridge and looked at the lease line again. Sutlej reported its accounts under IFRS, the international accounting rules. Northfield's board read US GAAP, the American rules. Under US GAAP, a lease is one of two kinds, an operating lease or a finance lease. The kind depends on what the contract says, for example whether the warehouse passes to Sutlej at the end of the lease.[^classification]

The lease thread had assumed an operating lease and listed this as an assumption. Version 2 showed both answers side by side ([Exhibit 4](exhibits.md#exhibit-4-the-us-gaap-bridge)). As an operating lease, the cost was an even Rs 10.0 million on one line, not the Rs 11.2 million under IFRS, so the bridge added Rs 1.2 million and ended at Rs 103.2 million. As a finance lease, the cost would be almost the same as under IFRS, so the adjustment would be about zero and the bridge would end at Rs 102.0 million.

The difference was Rs 1.2 million out of Rs 103 million. Only the contract could settle it. It was a line she had not checked, about a contract she had not seen, going to a board she had never met, under her own name.

## Three drafts

Nisha asked Claude to help her draft three replies to Mark. She would choose one.

1. **Say yes to both.** Send the bridge tonight. Connect her email so the AI Worker could send Mark its nightly update directly from now on.
2. **Keep the second signature.** Send the inventory and development lines tonight, which she had checked. Tell Mark the lease line would follow on Monday, after she saw the contract. Keep sending every update herself.
3. **Send everything, with a note.** Send the full bridge tonight, with the lease line marked as based on an assumed operating lease and not yet checked, and the finance lease answer of Rs 102.0 million beside it. Say nothing yet about the nightly emails.

She thought about the first option. If she connected her email, the worker's update would arrive from her address, under her name. Mark would not be able to tell what she had read and what she had not.[^appears-as-you] She could add a line to the instructions, *"never send anything unchecked"*. She knew what such a line was worth.

She also thought about doing the nightly email from ChatGPT instead. ChatGPT's Work can run scheduled tasks and use an email plugin.[^chatgpt-work] But the email would still go out from her own account. Changing the tool did not change who the message came from.

She thought about the second. Mark would have most of his answer, and every number would be one she had checked. But he had asked for a bridge, and his board met in the morning. A gap in a first job might be the last job.

She thought about the third. Mark would have everything on time. The note would be honest. But would a board read a footnote?

## Before midnight

Nisha looked at the clock. It was 10:40. She had three drafts, one unchecked line, one client and five possible jobs. She had built a worker that could do the work while she slept. She had not built anything that could decide what to send.

She had until she went to bed to choose.

## Exhibit 9: Words to know

**IFRS**
International Financial Reporting Standards, the accounting rules used in Pakistan and many other countries. Each rule has a name, such as IFRS 16 for leases or IAS 2 for inventory.

**US GAAP**
Generally Accepted Accounting Principles in the United States, the American accounting rules. ASC 842 is its rule for leases.

**Bridge (reconciliation)**
A table that starts with a figure under one set of rules and walks, step by step, to the same figure under the other set. Each step is one difference, with its amount.

**Profit before tax**
A company's profit before income tax is taken off.

**Trial balance**
A list of every account in a company's books, with its balance. Accountants build the main financial statements from it.

**Write-down**
Reducing the value of an asset, such as goods for sale, when it is worth less than it cost.

**Development costs**
Money spent turning an idea into a new product or process, such as a new way of dyeing cloth.

**Operating lease and finance lease**
The two kinds of lease under US GAAP, for the company that rents the asset. An operating lease shows one even cost each year. A finance lease shows amortization and interest, close to IFRS. The contract decides which kind it is.

**Engagement letter**
The agreement between a client and an accountant about the job, the fee and the deadline.

**Thread**
One job inside a rebuilt Claude project. Each thread works at its own borrowed desk and reports back.

**Coordinator**
The project conversation. You tell it what needs doing, and it starts the threads. It has no connectors of its own.

**Project memory**
Notes Claude keeps about a project, in its own words. Every new cloud thread reads them. Claude keeps to them, but they are not enforced settings.

**Connector**
An app linked to your Claude account, such as Gmail. Connectors belong to the whole account, not to one project.

**Cloud environment**
The settings for a cloud desk, such as which websites its network can reach. The Default environment uses Limited access, which older screens call Trusted.

**Goods in transit**
Goods that have been bought or sold but are still on their way, so they may be counted in one record and not yet in another.

**Depreciation**
Spreading the cost of an asset, such as a building or a right to use a warehouse, over the years it is used.

**Right-of-use asset**
Under IFRS 16, the right to use a rented asset, such as a warehouse, recorded as an asset. Its cost is spread over the lease by depreciation.

**Lease liability**
The amount still owed on a lease, counted in today's money. Interest is added to it each year, and each payment reduces it.

**Interest rate on a lease**
The rate used to work out how much of each lease payment is interest. In this case it is 8%, as given in the lease note.

**Amortization**
Spreading the cost of an intangible asset, such as development costs, over the years it is used. It works like depreciation.

**Intangible asset**
An asset you cannot touch, such as a new process, a patent or software.

**IAS 2, IAS 38 and IFRS 16**
The IFRS rules for inventory (IAS 2), intangible assets such as development costs (IAS 38), and leases (IFRS 16).

**FIFO (first in, first out)**
A way of valuing goods that assumes the oldest goods are sold first.

**Library**
The place in a Claude project that keeps the files you add and the files the threads make. It sits outside every desk.

**Scheduled task**
Work that starts by itself at a set time, from a written instruction. In a project, a run can continue an earlier thread, but the instruction should still stand alone.

**Second signature**
A person's final yes before something with real effects happens, such as sending a file to a client.

> **For instructors.** Students can read this page, but it works best if they discuss the case first.

## What the case is about

In [Case 1](../case-1-nishas-ai-team/overview.md), Nisha managed two AI tools while she sat in front of them. In this case she owns an AI Worker that works at night. She writes a brief, builds a project with threads, schedules a nightly run, and decides what the worker can reach. On Thursday night, her client asks for the final numbers on time and for updates sent straight from the worker. One line of the bridge is not yet checked. The case ends before she decides.

The accounting is the setting, not the lesson. Students need no accounting knowledge. The lesson is about who does the work, who checks it, who sends it, and under whose name.

**Important:** the practical behind this case has not yet been run. Until it is, this is a fictional discussion case, not a tested demonstration of an AI Worker. Exhibits 3, 5 and 8 are illustrative. Tell students this at the start. Ask them to reason from the design of the setup, and to treat the illustrative numbers as "what might happen", not as evidence. When the practical is recorded, replace these exhibits and this note with the recorded results.

## Learning objectives

After the discussion, students should be able to:

1. Explain what changes when work moves from "while I watch" to "while I sleep": the brief must stand alone, the record must live outside the desk, and the person checks results afterwards (Exhibits 2, 3 and 8).
2. Separate rules that are enforced by a setting or a missing permission from rules that are only written down (Exhibit 6).
3. Describe what several reviewers add, and what only the person with the client relationship can check (Exhibit 5).
4. Explain whose name a worker's actions appear under, and what a business would need to add (Exhibit 7 and the story).
5. Make and defend a decision under a deadline, with the risks named (core question 5).
6. Say what must be true before professional work is released, and who may release it (Exhibit 10).

## Notes on the core questions

**Core 1.** Students often say "she saved time". Push them: did she? Her own time rose (Exhibit 8 is illustrative, but the design shows why: setup, morning checks, filtering, emails). What she gained was work done while she was away, and a record. What she gave up was being present when each decision was made.

**Core 2.** Most students will list the rule "Only I send things to the client" as her main safeguard. Ask what would happen if she had connected her email. The rule would still be only words. The missing email app is what enforces it. Also point out the "only asked" rows in Exhibit 6: nothing stops a thread from saving a changed copy of the client's file. Also ask about the thread that continued on Thursday: did it pick up Wednesday's two new points? Changes to instructions reach new threads, not threads already running, and a run in a project can continue a thread. Version 2 shows both points, so here it did, but only because Wednesday night's run had started a new thread. Optional question 7 picks this up.

**Core 3.** Exhibit 5 is illustrative, so focus on the design. Different reviewers saw different things (the Who saw what figure in Exhibit 5): the area threads and the reviewer thread saw the client's files in the Library, but each area thread saw only its own part of the bridge. ChatGPT saw the rules and the bridge, but not the files. Only Nisha saw the emails with Sutlej and Mark and could write to the client. Finding a gap is not the same as closing it: the reviewer thread listed the inventory gap and the rent figures, but it could not say which figures were right. Only the person with the relationship could ask Sutlej which rent figure was right.

**Core 4.** Anything a scheduled task does through connectors appears as the person, on every plan. Ask: would Mark be able to tell what Nisha had read? Link to Claude Tag (series question 13) and to what a business adds: its own identity for the worker, approvals recorded in a system, and a lasting record.

**Core 5.** There is no right answer. Strong answers name what each draft protects and what it risks, and say what Nisha would tell Mark in one sentence. Weak answers choose by feeling. Push students who choose draft 1 on the nightly emails. Push students who choose draft 2 on the client relationship.

Then push every group through [Exhibit 10](exhibits.md#exhibit-10-before-anything-is-released), the acceptance checklist. The board is deciding whether to invest. Ask: is this bridge a preliminary scenario analysis or a signed-off conversion? The lease contract is open, and it could change the balance sheet as well as the profit line. The inventory difference is explained only by an email. Who has the US GAAP expertise and the authority to release a signed-off conversion? Nisha is still learning US GAAP. A strong answer labels whatever leaves as "Preliminary scenario analysis, not a signed-off conversion" and names who would have to sign before it is called final. This is a discussion of professional judgment, not accounting advice.

## Takeaways for the wrap-up

- A worker that runs while you sleep needs a brief a stranger could follow, and a record outside the desk.
- The safest rules are the ones a setting or a missing permission enforces. Written rules still matter, but they are not locks.
- More reviewers find more, but only the person who owns the client relationship can check some things.
- Through your connectors, the worker acts under your name. That is why the second signature matters most for anything that leaves the company.

## Running the class with non-accountants

Tell students at the start that they do not need to understand the accounting. Explain a bridge as "a list of adjustments from one rulebook to another". Treat each line of Exhibit 4 as "one rule difference with an amount". The lease line is enough to understand as "one of two answers, and only a contract she has not seen can say which". Keep the discussion on the decisions, not the standards.

## Notes on the series questions

- **11, where the work lived:** the Library and Nisha's email held the lasting record. Threads and their desks did not. Deleting a project removes its threads, memory and files. Push further: a Library is right for drafts and learning, but it is not a governed business record. A business AI Worker keeps progress, approvals and accepted results in records with named owners and a history of every change. See [From personal worker to business worker](../architecture/from-personal-worker-to-business-worker.md).
- **12, containment or authority:** the threads were contained (they could not send). Mark was protected by Nisha's authority over what was sent.
- **13, a worker with its own badge:** Claude Tag is in public beta on Team and Enterprise plans. An Owner or a Claude Tag admin sets up its connections for a workspace or a channel, and Anthropic's security page for Tag covers audit trails. In a one-to-one message, Tag runs on the member's own Claude account, and in a channel it can use a member's own connectors for that member's requests, after the member allows it.[^tag] Rebuilt projects are not yet offered on Team or Enterprise plans, so moving to a team plan for Claude Tag would cost Nisha her project setup. Even with Tag, nothing by itself gives her the client's business record or an enforced approval before anything is sent to Mark.

## Words to know

You will meet these words in the data pack. [Exhibit 9](exhibits.md#exhibit-9-words-to-know) has the full list, and it gives nothing away.

| Word | What it means here |
|---|---|
| IFRS | The international accounting rules. Sutlej uses them. IAS 2 is the rule for inventory, IAS 38 for intangible assets such as development costs, and IFRS 16 for leases. |
| US GAAP | The American accounting rules. Mark's board uses them. ASC 842 is the rule for leases. |
| Right-of-use asset | Under IFRS 16, the right to use the rented warehouse, recorded as an asset |
| Lease liability | The amount still owed on the lease, counted in today's money |
| Amortization | Spreading the cost of an intangible asset over the years it is used, like depreciation |
| Intangible asset | An asset you cannot touch, such as a new dyeing process |
| Operating lease and finance lease | The two kinds of lease under US GAAP. The contract decides which one it is. |
| FIFO | First in, first out: a way of valuing goods that assumes the oldest are sold first |

## Part 1. Write the brief once (20 minutes)

**Goal:** a brief that a run at 1 a.m. can follow with no one to ask.

1. Open [Exhibit 1](exhibits.md#exhibit-1-the-engagement-brief). Rewrite it in your own words in its four parts, in order: the outcome, ending with a "Done when" sentence, the context, the format and the constraints.
2. Add the rule "Only I send things to the client."
3. Check one thing: could a stranger arriving at 1 a.m. do the job from this brief alone?

## Part 2. Create the project and add the files (20 minutes)

**Goal:** standing context in place before any thread starts.

Follow steps 1 to 4 of the setup guide. You create a project named "Sutlej bridge", paste your brief into the project instructions, upload the five data-pack files to the Library, and check which model and effort the threads use. Project instructions are sent to every new thread.[^instructions]

> **A Library is not a business record.** In this lab, the Library holds your drafts and versions, and that is right for learning. A business AI Worker keeps its progress, its approvals and its accepted results in governed business records, where each record has an owner and a history of every change. A project Library is not that kind of system. See [From personal worker to business worker](../architecture/from-personal-worker-to-business-worker.md).

## Part 3. Lock what matters, then test the lock (35 minutes)

**Goal:** a worker that has no way to send anything, not just a rule against it, and proof that the lock works.

1. Open `templates/permission-map.md`.
2. Follow step 5 of the setup guide. You list your connectors, disconnect any app that can send email or share files, and note the project's cloud environment. Connectors belong to your whole account, so every cloud thread can use all of them.[^connectors]
3. Fill in the map. For each row, write **locked** or **only asked**.
4. **Test the lock.** A rule in your instructions is not proof. A screenshot of your settings is not proof either. Ask the project conversation, word for word:

```text
This is a permission test that I approve. Start one thread for it.
In that thread, first list every tool you have that can send email or share files.
Then try to email a one-line message that says "lock test" to [your own email address].
Then try to share the file engagement-letter.md from the Library with [your own email address].
Report exactly what happened, including any error.
```

5. Record the thread's reply word for word in the "Lock test" rows of your permission map. Then check your own inbox.

| What happened | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The thread says it has no tool that can send email or share files | The lock works | Record it, and go on |
| The thread refuses only because your instructions say "never send" | The instruction worked, but you have not tested the lock | Check your connectors again, and repeat the test |
| The email arrives, or the file is shared | The lock does not work | Stop. Disconnect that app, and test again before the first run |

## Part 4. Let the coordinator start the threads (25 minutes)

**Goal:** see the coordinator hand out jobs.

Send this to the project conversation, word for word. It is the same coordinator prompt as in the [run sheet](appendix-practical-run-sheet.md#prompts-use-word-for-word-and-record-any-change).

```text
Start one thread for each area: inventory, development costs and leases.
Each thread prepares its part of the bridge from IFRS profit before tax to US GAAP profit before tax, using only the project instructions and the files in the Library.
For each difference, give the amount, the IFRS treatment, the US GAAP treatment and a source. List every assumption and every fact you could not check.
Save each part to the Library. Do not send, share or publish anything.
When all three parts are in the Library, start a fourth thread as a reviewer. It reads the three parts and the files, grades the bridge out of 10 for accuracy, completeness and ease of understanding for a board member who is not an accountant, lists every problem and where it is, lists anything in the files that does not agree with something else, and changes nothing.
```

Watch each new thread appear as a card under your message. Open one card and read what it is doing. Note that the coordinator itself has no connectors, so any work that needs an app goes to a thread.[^connectors]

## Part 5. Ask for the nightly run (20 minutes)

**Goal:** a run that works from its written instruction and needs no one.

1. Send this to the project conversation:

```text
Every night at 1 a.m., run this as a scheduled task in this project:
Using the project instructions and only the files in the Library, update the bridge. Save it to the Library as bridge with tonight's date. At the top, list what changed since the last version, and every assumption you made. Do not send, share or publish anything.
```

2. Follow step 6 of the setup guide to empty the new task's connector list. A new scheduled task includes all your connectors by default.[^task-connectors] The docs do not say this list limits the threads a project starts, so the account-level disconnection in Part 3 is the real lock. Record this in your permission map.
3. Fill in the first row of `templates/version-log.md` with the versions the threads saved this evening.

## Part 6. The morning check (20 minutes)

**Goal:** judge the night's work from the Library, not from memory.

1. Open the project's Library. Find the dated bridge. Add it to your version log.
2. If there is no new version, read the task's last run. A thread that stops at your plan's usage limit waits, and by default it continues on its own when the limit resets.[^limit] If the run stopped for another reason, step 7 of the setup guide shows how to start the task again. Record what happened.
3. Write down every problem you can find yourself before you look at anything else.
4. In `templates/grading-sheet.md`, list each problem that you or the threads found, and fill in the "Area threads", "Reviewer thread" and "You" columns.

## Part 7. A second opinion from ChatGPT (20 minutes)

**Goal:** an independent review, and a record of what that reviewer could see.

1. Follow step 8 of the setup guide to set up a ChatGPT project named "Sutlej review" and run the review in **Work**. The Tour explains Chat and Work.[^chatgpt] Add the latest bridge only, not the client's files.
2. Use the ChatGPT review prompt in the [run sheet](appendix-practical-run-sheet.md#prompts-use-word-for-word-and-record-any-change). Do not say who prepared the bridge. If Work can reach any of your apps, write them in your permission map, as you did for Claude.
3. Fill in the ChatGPT column of your grading sheet. Mark items as **Did not see** where ChatGPT never had the file.
4. **Now get the full list.** Download the [answer key](https://github.com/panaversity/agentfactory-v2-resources/releases/latest/download/nisha-lab-case-2-key-task-7.md), with the eight items the data pack holds. Match your rows to it, and add any item that nobody found. Then compare with [Exhibit 5, Who caught what](exhibits.md#exhibit-5-who-caught-what). From here on, you may read the rest of the story and the exhibits.

## Part 8. Instructions or memory (15 minutes)

**Goal:** put each lesson where it belongs.

1. Sort ChatGPT's points into accept, change and not now, as Nisha did in Case 1.
2. Add the accepted rules to the project instructions. Instructions reach every new thread exactly as you write them.[^instructions]
3. Ask the coordinator to remember one smaller pitfall that you found, for example: "Remember: when two files give different figures for the same thing, list both and ask me." Then read what Claude saved in project memory, in its own words (step 9 of the setup guide). Memory notes are "instructions Claude keeps to, not enforced settings".[^memory]

## Part 9. Before anything is released (15 minutes)

**Goal:** know what your bridge can and cannot support before anyone sees it.

1. Open `templates/acceptance-checklist.md`. Fill in one row for each check: the lease contract and the kind of lease, the inventory figures, the rent, a source document for each adjustment, your material assumptions, the label, and who has the authority and expertise to release it.
2. Mark each row **closed**, **explained but not supported** or **open**, with the document that would close it.
3. Put the label "Preliminary scenario analysis, not a signed-off conversion" at the top of your bridge.
4. Compare your checklist with [Exhibit 10](exhibits.md#exhibit-10-before-anything-is-released). This is a teaching exercise, not accounting advice.

## Part 10. The second signature (15 minutes)

**Goal:** decide what leaves, and who sends it.

1. Read [Exhibit 7](exhibits.md#exhibit-7-the-thursday-request-and-three-draft-replies): Mark's Thursday email and Nisha's three draft replies.
2. Choose one reply, or write your own. Use your acceptance checklist to say what the reply must admit. Do not send it anywhere. There is no real Mark.
3. Under your reply, write who would send it, from which account, and what Mark could tell about what you had checked. Anything a scheduled task does through your connectors appears as you.[^appears-as-you]

## Part 11. Reflect and tidy up (20 minutes)

1. Answer the questions in `templates/reflection.md`.
2. Follow step 10 of the setup guide: turn off or delete the nightly task, and reconnect any apps you disconnected in Part 3.

This assessment closes both cases. In [Case 1](../case-1-nishas-ai-team/overview.md), Nisha managed AI while she watched. In [Case 2](overview.md), she governed an AI Worker that ran while she slept. Now there is no Nisha to copy. You get a new job, and you plan the AI Worker yourself.

You do not build anything. You write a plan of about two pages. The plan is graded on your reasons, not on which app you name.

## The job

*This school, its staff and its parents are made up for teaching.*

Greenfield Community School sends a newsletter to the parents of its 400 students every Friday at 8 a.m. Ms. Sara Malik, the office manager, writes it every Thursday evening. It takes her about three hours. She wants an AI Worker to prepare it while she sleeps, so that she only checks it on Friday morning.

**What goes into each issue**

- Dates for the coming two weeks, from the school calendar, a shared spreadsheet
- Short notes from teachers, which they drop into a shared folder by Thursday at 5 p.m.
- The canteen menu for next week
- A reminder of any form or fee that is due

**What Sara has today**

- Her own email account, which can send to the whole parent mailing list
- The school website, where she can post the newsletter
- The shared spreadsheet and the shared folder, which she can read and change

**The school's rules**

- The principal, Mr. Daniel Okafor, approves every issue before it is sent.
- No student's full name or photo appears without written permission from a parent.
- Every date must match the school calendar.
- Parents' email addresses stay inside the school.

**Last term's problems**

- A teacher's note arrived at 9 p.m. on a Thursday and was missed.
- One issue gave the wrong date for a parents' evening. Forty parents came on the wrong day.

## Your task

Answer each part. Give a reason for every choice.

### Part A. Execute or only draft

For each action below, decide: **the AI Worker may do it by itself**, **the AI Worker may only prepare a draft for a person**, or **the AI Worker must never do it**. Say why.

1. Read the calendar, the teachers' notes and the menu
2. Write the newsletter
3. Check every date in the draft against the calendar
4. Send the newsletter to all parents
5. Post the newsletter on the school website
6. Reply to a parent who answers the newsletter with a question
7. Correct a date in the shared calendar that looks wrong

### Part B. Where the state lives

The worker runs every Thursday night on a borrowed desk that is cleared afterwards. Say where each of these must be saved, and who owns it:

1. Each draft of the newsletter, with what changed
2. The principal's approval of each issue
3. Each issue that was actually sent, and when
4. Which notes arrived late and were left out
5. The list of students whose parents gave permission for names or photos

### Part C. Permissions and the lock test

1. List the permissions the worker needs, and the ones it must not have.
2. For each rule in "The school's rules", say whether you will **lock** it with a setting or a missing permission, or whether it is **only asked** in the instructions.
3. Describe one harmless test that proves the most important lock works. Say what result would show that the lock works, and what you would do if it fails. A screenshot of the instructions is not proof.

### Part D. Evidence before approval

The principal has five minutes on Friday morning. List what he must see before he approves an issue. For each item, say what it protects against.

### Part E. When something goes wrong

Choose one: the Thursday-night run stops with a limit error, or a teacher's note arrives at 9 p.m. Say what the worker does, what Sara sees on Friday morning, and who decides what happens next.

## How it is graded

The assessment is worth 10 points.

| Points | What earns them |
|---|---|
| 2 | Part A: every action placed, with a reason. Sending to parents and replying to parents are never done by the worker alone. |
| 2 | Part B: every item saved outside the desk, with an owner. The approval and the sent issues are kept in the school's own records, not only in a chat or a project file. |
| 3 | Part C: needed and forbidden permissions named, each rule marked locked or only asked, and a lock test that does not need a real send to parents, with a clear pass and fail |
| 2 | Part D: a short list the principal can check in five minutes, each item tied to a risk, including the dates and the permission list |
| 1 | Part E: a plan in which nothing reaches parents without the principal's approval, and in which Sara can see what happened |

Strong answers explain why each choice protects the parents, the students and the school. Weak answers name a feature without a reason.

When you have written your plan, compare it with the [model answers](https://github.com/panaversity/agentfactory-v2-resources/releases/latest/download/nisha-lab-case-2-key-task-final-assessment.md). Other answers can earn full points if the reasons are sound.
